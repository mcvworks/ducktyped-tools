What Is DNS Propagation?

Last updated: March 8, 2026

DNS propagation is the process by which updated DNS records spread across the global network of DNS resolvers. When you change a DNS record, the update does not take effect everywhere instantly -- it gradually propagates as cached copies of the old record expire.

How DNS Caching Creates Propagation Delays

The Domain Name System is designed for speed and resilience. To avoid querying authoritative nameservers for every single DNS lookup, resolvers at every level -- your browser, your operating system, your ISP's recursive resolver -- cache DNS responses. Each cached record is stored for a duration defined by its TTL (Time to Live) value, measured in seconds.

When you update a DNS record on your authoritative nameserver, the change is immediately available there. However, every resolver that already has the old record cached will continue serving it until the TTL expires. Only after the cached copy expires will the resolver fetch the updated record from the authoritative server.

This is why DNS propagation is not a single event but a gradual process. Different resolvers around the world cached the old record at different times, so their caches expire at different times.

What Determines Propagation Speed

Several factors affect how quickly DNS changes propagate globally:

TTL of the Previous Record

The most important factor is the TTL that was set on the old record -- not the new one. If your A record had a TTL of 86400 (24 hours), resolvers that cached it just before you made the change could serve the old value for up to 24 more hours. If the TTL was 300 (5 minutes), the maximum wait is much shorter.

Resolver Behavior

Not all resolvers strictly respect TTL values. Some ISP resolvers impose minimum TTL floors (sometimes 30 minutes or longer) to reduce their query volume. Others may cache records slightly beyond their TTL. Public resolvers like Google (8.8.8.8) and Cloudflare (1.1.1.1) generally respect TTLs accurately.

Multiple Caching Layers

DNS responses are cached at multiple levels: the browser, the operating system stub resolver, the local network resolver (such as a home router), and the ISP's recursive resolver. Each layer may have its own cache with its own expiry time. Even if one layer refreshes, another may still hold the old record.

Negative Caching

If a domain did not previously have a certain record type, resolvers may cache the negative response (NXDOMAIN or NODATA) according to the SOA record's minimum TTL. This means adding a brand-new record can also experience propagation delays if resolvers previously cached the fact that the record did not exist.

How to Minimize Propagation Time

While you cannot eliminate propagation delays entirely, you can minimize them with planning:

  1. Lower the TTL in advance -- at least 24-48 hours before making a DNS change, reduce the TTL on the record you plan to modify to a low value like 300 seconds (5 minutes). This ensures that by the time you make the change, most resolvers have the short-TTL version cached.
  2. Make the change -- update the DNS record on your authoritative nameserver.
  3. Verify propagation -- use DNS lookup tools to query multiple resolvers and confirm they are returning the new record.
  4. Raise the TTL -- once propagation is confirmed and the change is working correctly, increase the TTL back to a longer value (such as 3600 or 86400) to reduce query load on your nameservers.

Common Propagation Scenarios

Migrating to a New Web Host

When moving your website to a new server, you need to update your A record to point to the new IP address. During propagation, some users will reach the old server while others reach the new one. To handle this gracefully, keep the old server running until propagation is complete, and ensure both servers serve your site correctly.

Switching Email Providers

Changing MX records to point to a new email provider means that during propagation, some incoming emails will be delivered to the old provider and some to the new one. Plan the switch carefully, and monitor both mailboxes during the transition period.

Adding or Modifying TXT Records

SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records are stored as TXT records. When updating these, email authentication may be inconsistent during propagation. Lowering TTLs beforehand is especially important for email-related records, since authentication failures can cause messages to be rejected or sent to spam.
