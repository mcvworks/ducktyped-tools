What Is DMARC?

Last updated: March 8, 2026

DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance) is an email authentication protocol that builds on SPF and DKIM. It lets domain owners publish a policy that tells receiving servers how to handle emails that fail authentication, and provides reporting to monitor results.

Why DMARC Exists

SPF and DKIM each solve part of the email authentication problem, but they have gaps:

  • SPF authenticates the envelope sender (Return-Path) but not the From header that users see. An attacker can pass SPF by using their own Return-Path while spoofing the visible From address.
  • DKIM proves message integrity but does not tell receiving servers what to do when verification fails. A failed DKIM check might be logged but the message could still be delivered.
  • Neither SPF nor DKIM alone provides feedback to the domain owner about authentication failures.

DMARC closes these gaps by introducing alignment (requiring that the authenticated domain matches the visible From domain), policy (specifying how to handle failures), and reporting (sending authentication statistics back to the domain owner).

How DMARC Works

When a receiving server gets an email, it performs these DMARC-related steps:

  1. Extract the From domain -- the visible From header address determines which DMARC policy to look up.
  2. Look up the DMARC record -- query _dmarc.example.com for a TXT record.
  3. Check SPF -- evaluate SPF and check whether the authenticated domain aligns with the From domain.
  4. Check DKIM -- evaluate DKIM and check whether the signing domain (d=) aligns with the From domain.
  5. Apply policy -- if neither SPF nor DKIM passes with alignment, apply the DMARC policy (none, quarantine, or reject).
  6. Send reports -- generate aggregate and/or forensic reports based on the DMARC record's reporting addresses.

A message passes DMARC if either SPF or DKIM passes and aligns with the From domain. Both do not need to pass.

DMARC Record Syntax

A DMARC record is a DNS TXT record published at _dmarc.yourdomain.com:

_dmarc.example.com  TXT  "v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com; ruf=mailto:forensics@example.com; adkim=r; aspf=r; pct=100"

Key Tags

  • v=DMARC1 -- version identifier (required, must be first).
  • p= -- policy for the domain (required):
    • none -- monitor only. No action is taken on failures. Use this to gather data before enforcing.
    • quarantine -- failed messages should be treated as suspicious (typically sent to spam/junk).
    • reject -- failed messages should be rejected outright.
  • sp= -- subdomain policy. If omitted, the p= policy applies to subdomains too.
  • rua= -- address for aggregate reports (daily XML summaries). Multiple addresses can be comma-separated.
  • ruf= -- address for forensic (failure) reports (per-message details). Many providers do not send these due to privacy concerns.
  • adkim= -- DKIM alignment mode: r (relaxed, allows subdomains) or s (strict, exact match only).
  • aspf= -- SPF alignment mode: r (relaxed) or s (strict).
  • pct= -- percentage of messages to which the policy applies (default 100). Useful for gradual rollout.

Understanding Alignment

Alignment is the key concept that makes DMARC effective. It ensures that the domain authenticated by SPF or DKIM matches the domain in the visible From header.

SPF Alignment

SPF authenticates the Return-Path domain. For DMARC alignment, this domain must match the From header domain. In relaxed mode, bounce.example.com aligns with example.com. In strict mode, they must be exactly the same.

DKIM Alignment

DKIM authenticates the signing domain (d= tag). For DMARC alignment, this must match the From header domain. In relaxed mode, mail.example.com aligns with example.com. In strict mode, they must match exactly.

Implementing DMARC: A Phased Approach

  1. Start with p=none -- publish a DMARC record with p=none and an rua address. This enables monitoring without affecting email delivery.
  2. Analyze reports -- review aggregate reports to identify all legitimate sending sources and ensure SPF and DKIM are correctly configured for each.
  3. Move to p=quarantine -- once you are confident in your authentication setup, switch to p=quarantine. Consider using pct=10 initially and gradually increasing.
  4. Move to p=reject -- when reports confirm minimal false positives, enforce full rejection with p=reject; pct=100.

DMARC Reports

Aggregate Reports (rua)

Aggregate reports are XML files sent daily by receiving servers. They contain:

  • Source IP addresses that sent email claiming your domain.
  • The number of messages from each source.
  • SPF and DKIM authentication results for each source.
  • The DMARC policy applied (none, quarantine, reject).

These reports are essential for understanding who is sending email as your domain and whether legitimate senders are properly authenticated.

Forensic Reports (ruf)

Forensic reports provide per-message details about authentication failures, including redacted copies of the failed messages. However, many large email providers (including Gmail) do not send forensic reports due to privacy and volume concerns.

Common DMARC Challenges

Email Forwarding

When email is forwarded by an intermediate server, SPF typically fails because the forwarding server's IP is not in the original domain's SPF record. If DKIM also breaks (due to message modification), DMARC will fail. ARC (Authenticated Received Chain) is an emerging protocol designed to preserve authentication results across forwarding hops.

Third-Party Senders

Organizations often use third-party services (marketing platforms, CRM tools, transactional email services) that send email on their behalf. Each service must be configured to pass either SPF or DKIM with proper alignment. This often means adding include entries to SPF and configuring DKIM signing with the organization's domain.

Mailing Lists

Mailing lists frequently modify messages (adding footers, changing subjects, rewriting From addresses), which breaks both SPF and DKIM. Some mailing list software has been updated to handle DMARC by rewriting the From address. This is an ongoing challenge in the email ecosystem.
